Duke Dorm Energy Ranking Dashboard

A dashboard that shows Duke students how their dorm’s energy use compares, and how to lower it. Launching on Duke’s website Winter 2026/27.

In progressUpdates coming as the dashboard launches and I continue building.

Timeline
Jan 2026 – present
Role
UX/UI lead
Front-end
Partners
Duke Student Affairs Sustainability
2 master’s students
Tools
Figma
HTML/CSS/JS
Claude Code

The problem

The data existed, but nobody could see it.

Duke tracks electricity and water for every residence hall, but the numbers sat in spreadsheets that students and building managers never opened. You can’t change what you can’t see, so waste went unnoticed. Research suggested that showing it would help: live energy feedback has cut usage by around 4 to 7%, and Williams College saw a 4.2% drop after a short dorm competition.

Ideation

Fun for students, useful for managers

This started as a 3-person project in Duke’s Climate Tech Studio course, where I led the UX/UI and front-end. Since August 2026 I’ve designed and built it on my own with Duke Student Affairs Sustainability. The two audiences pulled in different directions. If students didn’t come back, nothing would change, and if building managers couldn’t read it, nothing would get fixed. So every idea had to be engaging and readable at once. I design in Figma first, then use Claude Code to build the front-end faster; I make the design decisions and test and edit everything it writes.

Iterations

Two early directions

The first version gave each student a profile with personal energy stats, which would have felt the most personal. But it needed accounts and room-level data Duke doesn’t have. The second paired a campus map with a ranked list of dorms. Dorm-level data was real and available, and dorm pride is a natural motivator, so it became the base of the final design.

TradeoffDropping profiles meant losing personal accountability. In exchange, students can use the site instantly with no sign-up, and every number shown is real. I also split one long page into separate pages: a few more clicks, but far less scrolling and clutter.

Design decisions

Making the ranking fair, and useful

Decision 1 · Rank each dorm against itself

Professors pointed out that ranking on raw usage isn’t fair, since dorms differ in age, equipment and number of beds. The oldest and biggest dorms would always come last, so their residents would have no reason to try. Ranking by month-over-month change means any dorm can climb by improving.

TradeoffA dorm that is already efficient has less room to improve, so it can rank lower than it deserves. To balance that, the graphs also show usage per student, so managers still see the absolute picture.

Decision 2 · Give students something to do

I watched 5 classmates use the site and surveyed 10+ students. They understood the data, but a ranking only told them there was a problem, not what to do about it. So I added a short to-do list of everyday energy-saving actions. I’ll run further testing once it launches.

TradeoffMore content on the main page competes with the data. I kept the list to five simple actions anyone can do from their room, so it adds a next step without crowding the rankings.

Design system

One small system so the data always reads the same way

Since the dashboard will live on Duke’s website, I built it on Duke’s navy and neutrals, then gave every color a single job so students never have to guess what it means.

Color

Every color has one job. Navy ties it to Duke, green always means better, red always means worse, and gold always means your dorm, so students never have to guess.

Duke Navy#2A387FHeaders, selected dorm panel

Typography

A rounded sans keeps it friendly for students, and a monospace for numbers lines up the digits so rankings and percentages are easy to compare at a glance.

DisplayNunito · 800 · 40Residence Hall Rankings
TitleNunito · 800 · 24Bassett Hall
BodyNunito · 400 · 16How does your dorm compare against other dorms in month-to-month energy spendage?

LabelSpace Mono · 400 · 11Electricity rank

Solution

Pick a dorm, see its rank, take action

The final design comes down to three parts, one for each step from seeing the problem to acting on it.

Pick your dorm

A hand-drawn campus map, because critiques showed people find their dorm faster visually than in a list.

See your rank

Ranked by month-over-month change so every dorm can compete. Dorms up more than 20% get flagged so building managers can spot problems fast.

Take action

A short to-do list turns the ranking into something students can do today.

Reflection

What I learned, and what’s next

Checking in with people outside the team, from professors to the director of sustainability, changed the design every time. The fair-ranking idea came straight from them. Designing for two audiences taught me that most decisions are tradeoffs, and the job is to make the cost small for both sides.

As I continue working on this project, there are many next steps to take. Measuring impact and garnering feedback are the top two priorities.

  1. How many users consistently engage with the platform?
  2. Have we seen a reduction unrelated to outside factors in electricity and water in residential halls?
  3. Do shorter-span challenges motivate more users?

For future integration into the Duke website, I’m working with the Duke Office of Information Technology. I hope to collaborate with them to analyze these metrics.

If we can demonstrate measurable behavioral change, we’ll know the platform is doing more than visualizing data; it is empowering students and building managers towards sustainable residential halls.

Beaufort Adopt-A-Drain

One mobile-friendly site where Beaufort, NC residents adopt a storm drain, report the trash they clean out, and kids follow a pirate scavenger hunt between painted drains. Done in the DukeEngage Marine Lab program.

Timeline
May – July 2026
Role
Designer on a team of 4
+ Duke CSI stakeholder
Program
DukeEngage
Duke Community Science Initiative
Tools
Figma
ArcGIS
Claude Code

The problem

Two separate tools, and neither one was working.

Duke’s Community Science Initiative ran Adopt-A-Drain on Anecdata, a general data platform that new volunteers found confusing. Because the trash form was hard to find, data came in unevenly, which made it hard to know which drains needed attention. The kids’ scavenger hunt lived in a separate app, GooseChase, which was expensive to run and didn’t hold kids’ attention. Volunteers had to learn two tools for one program, so they wanted everything on one site.

Iterations

From Figma to a working prototype

I started with low-fidelity Figma screens for the home page and each scavenger hunt stop, so we could agree on the structure with Duke CSI before spending time on visuals. Then I vibe-coded a quick prototype from the Figma draft with Claude Code to test the layout on a real phone, since most volunteers would use it outdoors on mobile. For the look, I took inspiration from Georgia’s Adopt-a-Drain branding and chose type and colors that felt professional enough for a town program but playful enough for kids.

We split the work by strength so everyone could contribute: our two teammates who weren’t interested in coding designed the scavenger hunt content in Canva, another teammate and I set up the ArcGIS maps and surveys, and I designed the website and built the hunt site.

Design decisions

Working within real limits

Decision 1 · Let the map choose the platform

My prototype and WordPress both gave me design freedom, but neither could support an interactive drain map, and finding a drain to adopt is the core of the whole program. Durham already ran a successful version on ArcGIS, so we built on its template. Data collection moved to Survey123 because it plugs into the same ArcGIS map, so every report is tied to the exact drain it came from. We kept the same questions Coastal Carolina uses so Beaufort’s data can be compared with theirs.

TradeoffArcGIS limited the visual design, and several of my early screens had to be reworked. I accepted that because a working, proven map mattered more to volunteers than custom styling. I added drain nicknames to bring back some personality and make adopting feel personal.

Decision 2 · Give the hunt its own site

ArcGIS couldn’t do the animations and playful feel a kids’ game needs, and a flat form would have lost kids’ attention just like GooseChase did. So I coded the scavenger hunt as its own mobile site, with animations to make it feel like an adventure. A wrong answer gives a hint instead of a dead end, so kids stay in the game, and each correct answer unlocks a fun fact about the sea creature on the drain so they learn why keeping drains clean matters.

TradeoffTwo sites is exactly what the Initiative wanted to avoid. To keep it feeling like one program, the sites link to each other, and buttons along the hunt send players straight to report trash.

Solution

Adopt it, clean it, explore it

The final design comes down to three parts, one for each way people take part.

Adopt a drain

Find an open drain on the map and name it, so adopters feel ownership and keep coming back.

Report what you clean

A short Survey123 form every two weeks, easy enough that data comes in consistently.

Follow the hunt

Riddles lead kids to the painted drains and teach them why keeping drains clean matters.

Reflection

What I learned, and what’s next

We handed the site off to Duke’s Community Science Initiative with a plan to advertise it to the community, and presented it to 20+ Beaufort residents and CSI staff. The program will continue with next year’s DukeEngage cohort.

Collaborating across skills

Through plenty of frustrating moments, we divided tasks by strength and came together for the toughest problems.

Reporting back

Regular check-ins with Duke CSI showed us which ideas were in scope for our timeline and budget.

Designing within constraints

Real technical limits are a different skill than Figma. Some of my early concepts had to be reworked once I knew what ArcGIS could support.

In future iterations, I’d test directly with Beaufort residents, not just Duke CSI, to validate whether the adoption flow and scavenger hunt actually land with end users.

– We would measure the metrics by tracking users through Survey123 and the ArcGIS map:

  • Adoption engagement rate (% of drains adopted vs adopted then let go)
  • Scavenger hunt engagement rate (% completing multiple stops vs. dropping off after one)
  • Scavenger hunt participants #
  • # of trash reports submitted

Overview

Snaily

The love of physical mail, turned digital: a web app for crafting and sending postcards to the people you miss. Try it live.

Event
SVSD Designathon
Team
Alayna Hu · frontend lead
Colin Zeng · backend lead
Platform
Mobile-forward web
Live
snaily-seven.vercel.app

The problem

Texting my parents is mostly logistics.

With a language barrier, texts between me and my immigrant parents tend to be short and practical, and it can be hard to show how much I care. Other first-generation students told me the same thing. Postcards show care because of the time spent personalizing them, so we built around three values from physical mail: customization, intention and connection.

Ideation

Keeping every screen personal

We sketched each part of the flow on paper and on the iPad, making sure every step had room for a personal touch. For example, a scrapbook-style photo section carries over the warmth of a real letter.

Iterations

Simpler, so parents can use it too

Our first prototype was generated with AI from a prompt listing the features we wanted, like an archive. We used it as a starting point, but the screens were overwhelming, especially for parents who are less comfortable with tech. The second added more customization but still felt impersonal. The final version keeps the flow simple and puts a small customizable element at every step, so it stays personal without asking much of the user.

TradeoffFewer screens meant cutting features, like a separate archive page up front. We moved the archive into the bottom bar so it is still there for people who want it, without getting in the way of sending a postcard.

Design decisions

Designing for two generations

Decision 1 · One photo step for two generations

Parents and kids arrange photos differently, so the photo step toggles between a familiar grid, like most photo apps, and a scrapbook-style pile that younger users can arrange freely. Tapping a photo lets you draw on it or add a Polaroid-style caption, so each photo carries a bit of the sender’s own story instead of just being attached.

TradeoffA toggle is one more control to understand. We made the familiar grid the default, so parents never have to touch it, and younger users can switch to the pile when they want.

Decision 2 · Real mail, or something new?

I wanted the photos separate from the postcard, like a real envelope. Colin felt that would just feel like texting a photo, and wanted them combined into one piece. We compromised with a toggle: photos paper-clipped to the card in an order you choose, or a free canvas where you drag them anywhere.

TradeoffTwo layouts means more to build and one more choice for the sender. We kept it because it protected what each of us cared about: the paper clip keeps the feel of real mail, and the canvas makes the postcard one complete piece.

Solution

Craft it, send it, open it

The final design comes down to three parts.

Craft the postcard

Pick a card, stamp and color, then write on the back, because choosing each detail is what shows care.

Make the photos yours

Draw on, caption and arrange photos so they tell a story instead of just sitting in a chat.

Keep an archive

Every postcard sent or received is saved, like a box of letters both sides can look back on.

Reflection

What I learned, and what’s next

Designing for both parents and kids taught me to build features that serve both sides rather than picking one. Critiquing constantly also showed me how small improvements add up.

Next, I’d test with real families to find where the flow still feels like friction. I’d also add audio so a postcard can be read aloud, since hearing a voice adds warmth, especially for my own parents. Translation between languages would tackle the language barrier directly, and themed cards like Chinese New Year would make it feel tailored to each family’s background.

To measure success, I’d track how many postcards each family sends over a month, how often a postcard gets a reply, and whether parents start sending their own, not just receiving them.
